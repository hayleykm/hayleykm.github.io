Parenting in today's drug landscape can feel overwhelming. Substances are stronger, more accessible, and often more unpredictable than maybe what you were used to. This page is designed to give you clear, honest, evidence-based information so you can talk with your children confidently, compassionately, and effectively. Below is a list of top 10 pressing questions that, as both a parent and user, I believe are likely to come up. They are also the kinds of conversations that, when approached with openness, care, and honesty, can build trust, reduce harm, and ultimately contribute to a healthier, more understanding world.

## 1) How do I talk to my kids about drugs?
Talking to your children about drugs should not be a single, serious "big talk," but rather an ongoing conversation that evolves as they grow. Start early, stay curious, and focus on listening as much as speaking. Ask open-ended questions like, "What are you hearing at school?" or "What do you think about vaping or cannabis?" The goal is not to lecture, but to understand their world.

Try to remain calm, even if you feel worried. Your reaction will determine whether they come back to you in the future. Avoid exaggeration or scare tactics: young people are quick to dismiss information that feels unrealistic.

When I was in the 10th grade, the adults chose this approach. Instead of creating intimacy and safety with smaller group conversations, they invited a former drug user to promote his biography, [The Agony of Ecstasy](https://www.irishtimes.com/news/health/to-ecstasy-and-back-1.35142). Ironically, the story did not make drugs feel less attractive to many of us. It made us question whether adults were telling us the full truth.

Offer honest, age-appropriate information and make it clear your primary concern is their safety and wellbeing, not punishment or panic. When your child feels heard rather than judged, they are far more likely to be open with you.

## 2) What is harm reduction and why am I hearing about it?
Harm reduction (often called HR) is an approach to drug education and public health that focuses on keeping people safe and reducing risks. It starts from a simple reality: some young people will experiment with substances. Rather than ignoring that possibility, harm reduction provides honest information and practical ways to reduce harm.

This can include drug-checking services that identify dangerous contaminants, education about safer choices, overdose prevention tools, and making sure people know how to get help quickly if something goes wrong.

A common misconception is that harm reduction encourages drug use. In fact, the goal is the opposite: to protect health and save lives. Research shows harm reduction can [reduce overdose deaths](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2022/0500/p454.html?utm), [prevent infections](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10524190/?utm), and [help people access care](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8541900/?utm).

For parents, harm reduction encourages open, realistic conversations. Instead of only relying on "just say no," it helps young people understand risks, make informed decisions, and know how to stay safer if they encounter substances.

## 3) If I think my child might be using drugs, what should I look out for?
Instead of looking for fixed "warning signs," which can overlap with normal adolescence, it is often more useful to notice patterns and changes in wellbeing. Sudden secrecy, mood swings, irritability, withdrawal from family activities, loss of interest in hobbies, or unexplained risk-taking may warrant attention.

If you notice concerns, approach with curiosity rather than accusation. For example: "I've noticed you seem more withdrawn lately. Is something going on?" This keeps the door open in ways that "Are you using drugs?" often does not.

## 4) How has the drug landscape changed since I was a youth?
Today's drug market is very different from previous generations. Substances are stronger, more potent, and more accessible than ever. For example, cannabis often contains higher levels of THC. Some new psychoactive substances (NPS) are designed to mimic cannabis, MDMA, cocaine, or LSD, but their composition changes frequently, making effects unpredictable.

Social media and online marketplaces can further increase exposure and normalize use. This is why drug literacy is increasingly important for both parents and young people.

## 5) What should I know about the Netherlands' drug policy?
Dutch drug policy is based on the Opium Act, which separates substances into two categories:

- Schedule I ("hard drugs"): heroin, cocaine, amphetamines, ecstasy, and GHB.
- Schedule II ("soft drugs"): cannabis, hashish, and some sedatives, considered less harmful (a view that has been [publicly contested](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(10)61462-6/abstract)).

Technically, possession, sale, and production of all drugs are illegal. In practice, the Netherlands applies a policy of toleration (*gedoogbeleid*): small amounts for personal use are usually not prosecuted (for example, up to 5g cannabis, 0.5g cocaine, or one ecstasy pill). These are practical thresholds, not formal legal rights, and enforcement can vary.

Overall, the Dutch approach is pragmatic and harm-reduction oriented, prioritizing public health, education, and access to support.

## 6) Drug testing services? Tell me more.
Drug testing is a (still underused) privilege and a potentially life-saving service, carried out by non-judgmental, knowledgeable, and approachable staff. The government supports drug checking because it helps protect public health by identifying dangerous substances and issuing timely warnings, for example through the Red Alert app. It also flags high-risk pills and monitors trends, such as increasing MDMA strength, so people can make more informed decisions.

More details and specific guidelines about testing can be found [here](https://www.jellinek.nl/en/alcohol-drugs-behavior/drug-testing-service/).

Many parents worry that these conversations might feel uncomfortable or confronting, but in reality, they are often very straightforward. To give you a sense of how approachable and professional this space is, I once took my boss along with me to do fieldwork at one of the city's drug testing services with some of my own drugs. If I can sit next to the person who deems me responsible enough to pay me while answering questions like "When did you last use this batch of pills? What were the side effects? Do you know who you got it from?", you, brave reader, can do this alongside your responsible child.

## 7) What is a good drug policy?
A good drug policy prioritizes health, safety, and evidence over punishment and stigma. It provides access to accurate information, prevention, treatment, and support services.

Effective policy reduces harm, protects young people, and addresses social and mental health factors that influence substance use. Fear and punishment alone do not prevent use, but education, connection, and accessible care can reduce risk and improve outcomes.

## 8) How can I support my child in making good choices?
Strong, trusting relationships are one of the most protective factors against harmful substance use. Open communication, clear boundaries, and consistent expectations help create safety and stability.

You can also help build decision-making skills. Talk through hypothetical scenarios. Discuss peer pressure and ways to say no. Encourage healthy outlets for stress and risk-taking, such as sport or creative activities.

When young people feel listened to, supported, and not immediately judged, they are much more likely to come to you when it matters.

## 9) My child is going to a festival for the first time. What should I know?
Festivals can be rich social spaces with music, community, and personal exploration. They can also involve real risks, so preparation matters.

Useful questions:

- Do they have a plan B to get home?
- Do they have enough money / payment options?
- Do they have warm clothes for the trip home?
- Do they know the venue policy and available care spaces?
- Do they know where first aid and security are?
- Are their phone and emergency contacts ready?

You can also discuss staying with trusted friends, looking out for each other, and making safer choices if substances come up. The goal is not to eliminate all risk, but to increase preparedness and safety.

## 10) Where can I get accurate and non-judgemental information on safer drug use?
Look for evidence-based, non-sensational sources. Public health organizations, youth mental health services, and harm reduction groups often provide practical information for parents.

Resources:

- [DrugsLab](https://www.youtube.com/c/Drugslab): Educational YouTube channel demonstrating drug effects and safer practices.
- [Poppi](https://poppi.amsterdam/): Experimental museum initiative exploring drugs through art and discussion.
- [Jellinek](https://www.jellinek.nl/en/alcohol-drugs-behavior/): Reliable information on alcohol and drugs, including risks and self-tests.
- [Unity](https://unity.nl/en/): Peer-led guidance on nightlife, alcohol, and drugs in the Netherlands.
- [Drug Science](https://www.drugscience.org.uk/): UK non-profit providing evidence-based drug research and policy guidance.
- [Erowid](https://www.erowid.org/): Global library of user reports and expert resources promoting responsible drug use.
- [Harm Reduction International](https://hri.global/): Global harm reduction resources and policy work.
